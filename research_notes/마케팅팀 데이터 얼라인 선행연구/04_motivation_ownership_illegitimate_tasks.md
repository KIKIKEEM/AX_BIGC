# Motivation, ownership and perceived legitimacy of tasks ("Do we really have to do this?")

Scope note for the report writer: the research environment blocked full-text retrieval (WebFetch/curl were refused by the network proxy for every publisher/PubMed/repository host tried). Everything below comes from search-engine abstracts, publisher metadata and secondary citations. Where a pooled effect size could not be read from the source itself, it is listed under Gaps rather than guessed. Evidence grades: A = meta-analysis/systematic review/guideline, B = RCT/large empirical study, C = small study/expert opinion/case, D = anecdote/unverified.

## Q1. What does research on "illegitimate tasks" (unnecessary and unreasonable tasks) say about stress, resentment and effort, and how can they be prevented?

### Takeaway
Illegitimate tasks are a well-established stressor in their own right. A 2026 meta-analysis (k = 141, N = 65,124) and a 2023 systematic review link them to worse attitudes, well-being and behaviour, beyond role conflict and justice. The two levers with the most support for prevention are (a) explaining *why* a task is necessary and acknowledging the burden it imposes, and (b) removing the "unnecessary" component, mostly dysfunctional tools and outdated routines. Highly intrinsically motivated employees are hit hardest.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition (foundational).** Tasks are illegitimate when they "violate norms about what an employee can properly be expected to do, because they are perceived as unnecessary or unreasonable". They threaten professional identity (the "stress-as-offense-to-self" framework). *Unnecessary* tasks could have been avoided or need not be done at all. *Unreasonable* tasks fall outside one's occupational role. Grade B (multi-study paper). — [Semmer et al. 2015, Work & Stress, "Illegitimate tasks as a source of work stress" (BORIS)](https://boris.unibe.ch/86240); definition also quoted in [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.904726/pdf)
- **Unique effect beyond related stressors.** Across several studies, illegitimate tasks predicted strain and well-being outcomes (e.g., burnout, resentment toward the organization) "beyond the effects of other predictors", including role conflict and organizational justice. Grade B. — [Semmer et al. 2015 (BORIS record)](https://boris.unibe.ch/86240); [boris.unibe.ch/48334](https://boris.unibe.ch/48334)
- **Mechanism.** Illegitimate tasks drain mental and emotional energy, which leads to emotional exhaustion. A review reports that the positive association with burnout is "very stable, regardless of the research context". Resentment, and possibly guilt or shame through self-devaluation, are the typical emotional reactions. Grade B, via secondary citation. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021 (citing Semmer et al. 2015)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.739593/xml); [PMC11274157](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11274157/)
- **Newest meta-analysis.** Li & Mostafa (2026), *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 31(4), 279–301, DOI 10.1037/ocp0000435. It pooled k = 141 studies and N = 65,124 participants; the accepted manuscript reports k = 145 and N = 66,387, so the published figures should be used. Outcomes cover:
  - attitudes: affective commitment, job satisfaction, engagement, turnover intention
  - well-being: negative affect, burnout, work–family conflict
  - behaviour: job performance, innovative behaviour, OCB, CWB, withdrawal

  It also compares unreasonable vs. unnecessary tasks and tests power distance, age, rating source and time lag as moderators. Grade A. The pooled ρ values were NOT retrieved (see Gaps). — [PubMed 42347775](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42347775/); [White Rose accepted manuscript](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/239995/)
- **Systematic review.** Ding & Kuvaas (2023), *Work & Stress* 37(3), 397–420. It organizes antecedents into leadership, workplace, individual and job factors, and outcomes into emotions, attitudes, behaviour, health and relationships. Abusive supervision is linked to more unreasonable tasks. Supportive messages from leaders (acknowledgment, appreciation, explanation) can reduce how illegitimate employees judge a task to be. Grade A (systematic narrative review). — [Ding & Kuvaas 2023, Work & Stress](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02678373.2022.2148308)
- **A second review (2025)** found that illegitimate tasks affect well-being and stress uniquely, even when conceptually related factors are controlled. It cautions that the heterogeneity and quality of the included studies limit generalizability. Grade A-/B (lower-tier journal). — [Iqbal et al. 2025, J. of Economics & Management](https://www.sbc.org.pl/Content/896662/2025-47-02_Iqbal_et_al.pdf)
- **What makes a task feel "unnecessary".** A Finnish content analysis of employees' free-text reports found two leading categories for unnecessary tasks: impractical or outdated working habits, and dysfunctional technology. For unreasonable tasks, "tasks outside one's occupational role" made up 78% of reports. Grade C (qualitative content analysis; N not verified). — [Jyväskylä University repository](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstreams/9ba295e0-062a-44e8-b6ae-ecad4a577b92/download)
- **The most motivated suffer most.** In 604 UK/US faculty, illegitimate tasks were linked to strain and turnover intention, and these links were stronger for highly intrinsically motivated staff. The effect on satisfaction was only marginal. Grade B. — [Fila, Semmer & Kern 2023, Occupational Health Science](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41542-022-00140-w)
- **Link to SDT motivation is bidirectional.** A three-wave longitudinal study of about 700 Finnish workers found that illegitimate tasks predicted rising amotivation and falling autonomous motivation over time. Autonomous motivation in turn predicted fewer *perceived* illegitimate tasks later. The author recommends supporting autonomous motivation and the workplace atmosphere. Grade B. — [Karkkola 2026, Scandinavian J. of Psychology (PMC12790102)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12790102/); [UEF press release](https://www.uef.fi/en/article/autonomous-motivation-can-help-reduce-perceived-illegitimate-tasks)
- **Intrinsic motivation and job satisfaction.** Illegitimate tasks reduce intrinsic motivation and job satisfaction, with moderated mediation by gender and effort–reward imbalance. Grade B (empirical survey; coefficients not retrieved). — [Omansky et al. 2016, PMC5116474](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5116474/)
- **Attributions matter, and the two types differ.** In 432 engineers (mixed methods), unreasonable and unnecessary tasks drew different attributions and different negative emotions. Hostile attribution bias amplified negative emotions, particularly for unreasonable tasks. The authors argue unreasonable tasks threaten self-worth more than unnecessary ones. Grade B. — [Pindek et al. 2019, Work & Stress (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326303674_Illegitimate_tasks_are_not_created_equal_Examining_the_effects_of_attributions_on_unreasonable_and_unnecessary_tasks)
- **Supervisor support buffers unreasonable tasks only.** Task unreasonableness was less strongly tied to depressive symptoms when perceived supervisor support was high. No such interaction appeared for unnecessary tasks. Grade B/C. — [Fila & Eatough 2020, Psychological Reports](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033294119874292) (described in citing literature)
- **Prevention through explanation and acknowledgment.** Minei, Eatough & Cohen-Charash (2018), *Management Communication Quarterly* 32(3), 374–397, frames the management of illegitimate task requests through explanation and acknowledgment as discursive leadership. A 2024 public-sector study cites it, together with Sias & Duncan 2019, for the point that managers who express recognition and gratitude and who clarify the purpose of a task reduce the perception of illegitimacy, and that leaders who explain the reasons behind tasks make employees more likely to accept them. Grade C (communication-based approach; the original design was not verified). — [Public-sector study, Nordic Psychology 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19012276.2024.2444932); [Minei, Baruch faculty profile](https://www.baruch.cuny.edu/profiles/faculty/Elizabeth-M-Minei)
- **Leadership as a moderator in the public sector.** Illegitimate tasks predicted lower work engagement, more stress and higher turnover intention, with leadership moderating these links. Grade B (sample size not verified). — [Nordic Psychology 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19012276.2024.2444932)
- **Asian/face context.** In Chinese samples, illegitimate tasks predicted counterproductive behaviours such as destructive voice and time theft (via moral disengagement, in 258 supervisor–subordinate dyads). A "face" perspective study argues that they elicit both proactive aggression and passive resistance. Grade B/C. — [J. Business Ethics 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-021-04848-7); [Jiang 2025, Asian J. Social Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajsp.70004?af=R)

### Inferences
- "Do we really have to do this too?" is, in this literature's terms, a *legitimacy appraisal*. Team members are judging data collection as **unnecessary**: the purpose is unclear, it duplicates other work, or the tools are clunky. They may also judge it as **unreasonable**: "this is an analyst's job, not a content marketer's." The research says this appraisal carries its own stress and resentment cost, beyond workload.
- The leading triggers for "unnecessary" (outdated routines, dysfunctional tech) are directly fixable design problems, and are largely the leader's responsibility.
- Explanation plus acknowledgment is the cheapest evidence-backed intervention. The explanation covers why this data is needed and which decision it feeds. The acknowledgment is something like "I know this is extra work on top of your campaign load."
- The finding that intrinsically motivated staff are most harmed implies that the team's best people may resist the most. Their resistance signals perceived pointlessness, not laziness.
- The bidirectional SDT link (Karkkola 2026) means that raising autonomous motivation (Q2) also lowers perceived illegitimacy. The two levers reinforce each other.
- **Application to the marketing team:** Audit each data-collection request with two questions per role (performance, content/SNS, brand/CRM):
  1. Necessary: does it feed a decision this person cares about?
  2. Reasonable: is it within this person's professional identity?

  Then cut or automate whatever fails question 1, and reassign or reframe whatever fails question 2. For example, frame it as "content performance diagnostics that make *your* content better", not "reporting for the lead". When a task truly must stay, explain its downstream use and acknowledge its cost explicitly. In a Korean, higher-power-distance team, members may comply outwardly while resenting the work, which matches the "passive resistance" pattern in the face/CWB studies. So silence should not be read as buy-in.

### Gaps
- Pooled ρ values from Li & Mostafa (2026) were not retrievable: magnitudes by outcome, whether unreasonable or unnecessary tasks predict more strongly, and whether power distance moderates. This matters for the Korean context; the full text is at DOI 10.1037/ocp0000435.
- No randomized intervention study testing "explanation/acknowledgment" or task-redesign interventions on illegitimate-task perceptions was found. The prevention evidence is correlational or communication-based (grade C).
- No Korean-sample study of illegitimate tasks was found in this search pass.
- A 2026 study on "the double-edged sword effect of empowering leadership on illegitimate tasks" ([PMC12832405](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12832405/)) appeared in results, but its findings could not be read.

## Q2. What do Self-Determination Theory workplace meta-analyses say about autonomy, competence and relatedness and work quality?

### Takeaway
Multiple large meta-analyses (Van den Broeck et al. 2016; Slemp et al. 2018, 2024; Hagger & McAnally Star 2026) consistently show that support for autonomy, competence and relatedness, from leaders and from peers, raises need satisfaction and autonomous motivation. These in turn relate positively to performance and effort, and strongly to well-being. Intrinsic motivation is a medium-to-strong predictor of performance and appears to matter most for *quality*, while incentives drive *quantity*.

### Cited Findings
- **Van den Broeck, Ferris, Chang & Rosen (2016)**, *Journal of Management* 42(5), 1195–1229. This meta-analysis covers 99 studies and 119 samples. Each of the three needs was:
  - positively related to job satisfaction and affective commitment, and negatively to turnover intention
  - positively related to performance measures and effort, and negatively related to deviance

  The three needs explain incremental variance over one another. Grade A. — [Journal of Management DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0149206316632058); [full PDF on selfdeterminationtheory.org](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2016_VandenbroeckFerrisEtAl_a-review-of-self-determination-theory.pdf)
  - Secondary sources report that autonomy was the most influential need for work motivation, explaining up to 42% of the variance in intrinsic motivation. Treat this as a secondary report (grade A source, unverified number). — via [search summary of citing works](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1216450/xml)
- **Slemp, Kern, Patrick & Ryan (2018)**, "Leader autonomy support in the workplace: A meta-analytic review", *Motivation and Emotion* 42(5), 706–724. It drew on 754 correlations from 72 studies (83 samples, N = 32,870). Leader autonomy support correlated strongly and positively with autonomous work motivation and was unrelated to controlled motivation. It predicts well-being, engagement and positive work behaviour through need satisfaction and autonomous motivation. Effects were NOT moderated by country, source of support or measurement. Grade A. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-018-9698-y)
  - The same paper reports leader autonomy support → need satisfaction correlations of autonomy ρ = .65 (k = 13), competence ρ = .38 (k = 13) and relatedness ρ = .39 (k = 14), as cited in search summaries. Grade A, number from a secondary summary. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-018-9698-y)
- **Slemp, Field, Ryan, Forner, Van den Broeck & Lewis (2024)**, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 127(5), 1012–1037. It pooled 4,561 effect sizes from 881 samples. Key results:
  - Interpersonal support for each need is strongly tied to satisfying that need and to reduced need frustration.
  - Support is strongly related to well-being, with small-to-moderate positive associations with performance.
  - **Lateral (peer) support explained incremental variance beyond vertical (leader) support.**
  - The link between autonomy support and autonomous motivation weakened with individualism, i.e., it was stronger in less individualistic cultures.

  Grade A. — [SDT site PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2024_SlempFieldRyanEtAl_Interpersonal.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379926332_Interpersonal_supports_for_basic_psychological_needs_and_their_relations_with_motivation_well-being_and_performance_A_meta-analysis)
- **Hagger & McAnally Star (2026)**, "Self-Determination Theory and Workplace Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis", *Stress and Health* 42, e70151. This is a multilevel meta-analysis with k = 192 plus a structural path model. Leader need support → need satisfaction → autonomous motivation → adaptive outcomes, with negative indirect effects on maladaptive outcomes. **The effects were larger for corporate employees than for public-service workers.** Grade A. — [PMC12887546](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12887546/); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/smi.70151)
- **Deci, Olafsen & Ryan (2017)**, "Self-Determination Theory in Work Organizations: The State of a Science", *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 4, 19–43. When employees perceive autonomy support, they also tend to feel more connected and more effective. Social settings that support autonomy and the other needs consistently predict motivation, performance and wellness. Grade B (authoritative narrative review, not systematic). — [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113108)
- **Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford (2014)**, "Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives jointly predict performance: A 40-year meta-analysis", *Psychological Bulletin* 140(4), 980–1008. Intrinsic motivation is a medium-to-strong predictor of performance across school, work and physical domains. Incentive salience moderates this, and incentives and intrinsic motivation are "not necessarily antagonistic". Grade A. — [SDT site PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/2014_Cerasoli_Intrinsic.pdf)
  - The same authors report that intrinsic motivation predicts more variance in performance **quality**, while incentives predict more performance **quantity**. This wording was confirmed only through a related conference abstract and a ResearchGate citation, not the 2014 full text. — [ResearchGate (Cerasoli et al.)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273978668_Incentives_Intrinsic_Motivation_and_Performance_A_Meta-Analysis_and_Theoretical_Reconciliation); [ScienceForWork summary](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/incentives-motivation-performance/)

### Inferences
- The leader's complaint has two parts: low effort ("annoying") and analysis that falls short "in level and direction". The first maps onto controlled versus autonomous motivation. The second maps onto *quality* of performance, which per Cerasoli et al. depends more on intrinsic/autonomous motivation than on mandates or incentives. Mandating more reporting will likely raise quantity (more sheets filled) without raising analytic quality.
- Autonomy support does not mean "no structure". In SDT it means giving a rationale, acknowledging feelings, and offering choice in *how*. This overlaps with the explanation and acknowledgment lever from Q1.
- The competence need is under-addressed in many data initiatives. If members do not feel able to analyze, they will experience the task as threatening rather than meaningful. Building analytic competence (templates, worked examples, feedback on analyses) is a need-support intervention, not just training.
- Peer (lateral) support adds explanatory power beyond the leader (Slemp 2024). Peer review of analyses and pairing performance and content marketers are evidence-consistent ways to build relatedness and competence together.
- The stronger autonomy-support effect in less individualistic cultures (Slemp 2024) and in corporate settings (Hagger 2026) suggests these levers should work at least as well in a Korean corporate marketing team as in Western samples.
- **Application to the marketing team:** Restructure the data initiative so that:
  - **Autonomy:** each member chooses which questions or metrics in their own channel they will investigate, within the leader's shared goal.
  - **Competence:** the leader provides analysis templates and short coaching on "what a good insight looks like".
  - **Relatedness:** analyses are shared in a regular team insight review where peers build on each other's findings.

  Expect gains in analysis *quality* to come from this, not from tighter deadlines or reporting quotas.

### Gaps
- Performance-specific ρ values per need from Van den Broeck et al. (2016), and the workplace-subgroup performance ρ from Slemp et al. (2024), were not retrieved.
- No SDT meta-analytic estimate specific to marketing or data-analysis teams was found.
- No Korea-specific SDT workplace meta-analysis was found. Cultural moderation evidence comes from cross-national moderators.

## Q3. What does research on psychological ownership, the IKEA effect and participation in change say about co-creating systems?

### Takeaway
People value, and identify with, what they help build. The IKEA effect is robust (meta-analytic d = 0.57) but holds only when the effort leads to *successful completion*. Workplace psychological ownership predicts positive attitudes and performance but also has a territorial dark side. Ownership of one's *own* idea makes people blind to suggestions that subtract from it, and this applies to leaders as well. Participation effects on performance are real but small once self-report inflation is removed.

### Cited Findings
- **Original IKEA effect.** Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012), *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 22(3), 453–460. In four studies, people who assembled IKEA boxes, folded origami or built Lego sets valued their creations more:
  - Builders rated their amateur creations about as highly as experts' creations, and expected others to agree.
  - **The effect dissipated when participants built and then destroyed their creation, or failed to complete it.**

  Grade B (lab experiments). — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-ce76-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b); [HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=41121)
  - The magnitude of the willingness-to-pay premium for the storage box is reported inconsistently in secondary sources as 62%, 63% or 64%. Grade D until checked against the original. — [neoacademic (62%)](https://neoacademic.com/2011/09/22/unfolding-the-ikea-effect-why-we-love-the-things-we-build); [PiXL (64%)](https://www.pixl.org.uk/_site/data/files/5E01164D37D7B43658D6068FC511D16A.pdf)
- **IKEA effect meta-analysis.** Demetriades, Walter (and Pelled per ResearchGate), "Labor Leads to Love, Right? A Meta-Analysis of the IKEA Effect", *Psychology & Marketing* 43(2), 388–400. It was published online on 14 Oct 2025 and covers 55 studies with N = 5,454. Self-assembly labor had a moderate effect on valuation (**d = 0.57**), with additional effects on liking, self-concept and sense of ownership. The effects were robust across moderators such as customization and tangibility. Grade A. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mar.70064); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396474022_Labor_Leads_to_Love_Right_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_IKEA_Effect)
- **Workplace psychological ownership meta-analysis.** Zhang, Liu, Zhang, Xu & Cheung (2021), *Journal of Management* 47(3), 745–770, covering 141 studies over 20 years:
  - Psychological ownership relates to employees' attitudinal and performance outcomes.
  - It also relates to "dark-side" outcomes such as territorial behaviours.
  - Safety-related antecedents (justice, trust, perceived organizational support, relational closeness) are emerging drivers.

  Grade A. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0149206320917195); [Southampton repository](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/484215)
- **HR antecedents meta-analysis.** Renz (2024), *American Business Review*, covering 139 studies with N = 351,919. Compensation and benefits, **job and work design**, and **communication practices** foster psychological ownership. Societal culture significantly strengthens these relationships. Grade A (lower-tier journal). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ris/ambsrv/0104.html)
- **Consumer psychological ownership meta-analysis.** Nann (2025) found that "investing oneself in the target" and "intimately knowing the target" have particularly strong effects on ownership. Grade A, but this is a conference proceeding, not a peer-reviewed journal article. — [EMAC 2025 proceedings](https://proceedings.emac-online.org/pdfs/A2025-126452.pdf)
- **Narrative review.** Dawkins, Tian, Newman & Martin (2017), *Journal of Organizational Behavior*: a narrative review of psychological ownership and research agenda. Grade B/C. — [eCite UTAS](https://ecite.utas.edu.au/103055)
- **Ownership of ideas blinds people to subtractive feedback.** Baer & Brown (2012), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 118, 60–71:
  - People with strong ownership of an idea were "blind" to suggestions that diminish parts of it, and receptive to feedback that builds on it *regardless of its quality and usefulness*.
  - The mechanism is felt loss and negative affect.
  - The authors recommend encouraging *collective* ownership of ideas and a feedback culture.

  Grade B (experiments). — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120607142246.htm); [IO at Work summary](http://www.ioatwork.com/employees-embrace-or-resist-feedback/)
- **Participation meta-analytic evidence.** Wagner (1994), *Academy of Management Review* 19(2), 312–330, reviewed 11 earlier reviews and reanalyzed Cotton et al. (1988):
  - Participation has small but statistically significant positive correlations with performance and satisfaction, with somewhat stronger effects on satisfaction.
  - Much of the larger effect seen in earlier reviews came from single-source self-report ("percept–percept") designs.
  - Cotton (1995) disputed this, and Wagner & LePine (1999) added further meta-analytic evidence.

  Grade A (contested). — [Wagner & LePine 1999](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.2466/pr0.1999.84.3.719); [AMJ meta-analytic review of participation](https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/255942); [summary notes](https://sites.google.com/site/aristhesis/home/notes/wagner-1994-participation-s-effects-on-performance-and-satisfaction-a-reconsideration-of-research-evidence)
- **Reactions to change.** Oreg, Vakola & Armenakis (2011), *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science* 47(4), 461–524, a 60-year review of quantitative studies. It organizes antecedents of change recipients' reactions into pre-change factors (e.g., recipients' characteristics, internal context) and change factors (process, perceived benefit or harm, content). The *process* of change, including how it is implemented, is a distinct antecedent class. Grade A (systematic review of quantitative studies). — [Oreg, Vakola & Armenakis 2011 PDF (HUJI)](https://bschool-en.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/businesshe/files/oreg_vakola_armenakis_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- The leader holds a framework (purpose → data → analysis → action) that they built, so they experience it through the IKEA effect. Team members did not build it, so they experience it as an external imposition. Co-creating the data system closes this gap: which metrics, which template, which review ritual. This lets members "invest the self" and "intimately know" it, which are the strongest antecedents of ownership in Nann's consumer meta-analysis.
- The "completion" boundary condition is critical. Co-creation works only if members see their contribution reach a finished, *used* outcome: a decision made, a campaign changed. If the data they build is never used ("built then destroyed"), the ownership effect disappears and the task reverts to "unnecessary" (Q1).
- Baer & Brown carry a warning for the leader. Strong ownership of one's *own* framework makes a person selectively deaf to team suggestions that cut parts of it. That deafness signals to the team that participation is cosmetic. Leaders should explicitly invite *subtractive* suggestions ("what should we stop collecting?").
- The territoriality dark side (Zhang et al. 2021) suggests designing for *collective* team ownership of the shared dataset or dashboard, not individual fiefdoms ("my channel's data").
- Participation alone gives small performance gains (Wagner 1994). It works mainly through satisfaction and acceptance, and should be paired with competence building and real decision rights rather than token consultation.
- **Application to the marketing team:** Run a short co-design workshop, which the leader is well placed to facilitate as a design-thinking educator:
  1. Members map which decisions in their own role would benefit from data.
  2. They propose which metrics to drop.
  3. They prototype a one-page insight template together.
  4. They commit to a first "data → action" cycle whose result is visibly used within weeks.

  The leader keeps the *why* (purpose/goal) fixed but lets the team own the *how*.

### Gaps
- Pooled ρ values for psychological ownership with performance and job satisfaction (Zhang et al. 2021) were not retrieved.
- Dirks, Cummings & Pierce (1996), on when ownership promotes or resists change (self-initiated vs. imposed), could not be verified in this pass, so no claim is made from it.
- No meta-analysis isolating *employee participation in designing work systems/tools* → adoption quality was found. Oreg et al. (2011) treats process factors broadly.
- No study of the IKEA effect in *organizational process/system co-design*, as opposed to consumer products, was found. Its extension to work systems is an inference.

## Q4. What does technology acceptance research (TAM: perceived usefulness and ease of use) say about adopting data tools and processes?

### Takeaway
Across meta-analyses, perceived usefulness (performance expectancy) is the strongest and most consistent predictor of intention to use a technology. Correlations are typically around r ≈ .50, ahead of ease of use at about .45–.47. Usefulness is driven mainly by job relevance, output quality and result demonstrability, so a data process gets adopted when people see that it improves *their* work outputs. Mandated use changes the dynamics but does not remove the need for perceived usefulness.

### Cited Findings
- **King & He (2006)**, "A meta-analysis of the technology acceptance model", *Information & Management* 43(6), 740–755. It pooled 88 studies with more than 12,000 observations and concluded that TAM is "a valid and robust model". The perceived usefulness, ease of use and behavioural intention measures are highly reliable. Grade A. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378720606000528); [author PDF](http://www.sietmanagement.fr/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/KingHe-copie.pdf)
- **Marikyan, Papagiannidis & Stewart**, "Technology acceptance research: Meta-analysis", *Journal of Information Science* (2023 online; 2026 issue). Performance expectancy had the largest total effect on attitude (**0.53**, 95% CI ≈ 0.16–0.90, imprecise), followed by perceived usefulness (**0.43**) and perceived enjoyment (0.41). Grade A. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01655515231191177); [Bristol repository](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/technology-acceptance-research-meta-analysis/)
- **UTAUT meta-analysis (37 studies).** Performance expectancy had the strongest correlation with behavioural intention (effect size **.536**). Another UTAUT synthesis found that only the performance expectancy → intention relationship was strong. ### Inferences
- A data-collection or analysis *process* functions like a technology being adopted. "Is this annoying?" is perceived ease of use. "Do we really have to?" is perceived usefulness, and for this team it is a usefulness deficit first.
- The TAM2/TAM3 drivers of usefulness map onto concrete leader actions:
  - **Job relevance:** each metric ties to that person's channel KPIs.
  - **Output quality:** the analysis visibly produces better recommendations.
  - **Result demonstrability:** show a before/after example where data changed a campaign decision and its result.
- Mandating the process (a lead's directive) can produce compliance through social influence early on. But meta-analytic evidence that performance expectancy dominates intention suggests that sustained, high-quality use still depends on members believing it helps *them*. This connects to the quality and quantity distinction in Q2.
- Ease-of-use barriers (anxiety, low self-efficacy with analytics tools) sit on the ease-of-use path. Pre-built templates, automated data pulls and short skill sessions lower that barrier. Combined with Q1, a clunky tool turns the task from "useful but effortful" into "unnecessary".
- **Application to the marketing team:** Before asking for more data work, run a "usefulness proof":
  1. Pick one member's real problem (e.g., an SNS content format underperforming).
  2. Co-analyze it using the proposed process.
  3. Show the decision it led to and the result.

  Then remove friction (automate collection where possible, standardize a single template) before scaling. Track adoption by whether insights change actions, not by sheets submitted.

### Gaps
- King & He (2006) pooled path coefficients (PU→BI, PEOU→BI, PEOU→PU) were not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis specific to *marketing analytics/data-process adoption by marketing staff* was found. Inference from general TAM/UTAUT is required.
- Detailed results of Brown et al. (2002) on which TAM paths hold under mandate were not retrieved.
- Recent (2020–2026) meta-analyses on AI/analytics tool adoption at work were not systematically searched in this pass. An arXiv extended-UTAUT study on employees' AI adoption appeared ([arXiv 2510.15142](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15142)) but was not reviewed and is not peer-reviewed.
