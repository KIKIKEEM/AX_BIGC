# Empirical research on marketing analytics deployment and marketing-metrics use

*Method note: WebSearch only. WebFetch failed for every primary site tried (DNS ENOTFOUND for informs.org and cmosurvey.org), and direct PDF download through the proxy returned 403. All findings below come from abstracts, publisher or university repository records, press releases and search-result snippets, not from the full papers. Evidence grades: A = meta-analysis, systematic review or guideline; B = RCT, quasi-experiment or large empirical study; C = small study, conceptual paper, expert opinion, industry survey or case; D = anecdote or unverified. No A-grade evidence (meta-analysis) on marketing analytics deployment turned up in this search.*

## Q1. What did Germann, Lilien & Rangaswamy (2013, IJRM) find about the performance effects of deploying marketing analytics, and about its antecedents (top-management advocacy, analytics culture)?

### Takeaway
In a survey of 212 Fortune 1000 senior executives, firms that deployed more marketing analytics showed better performance: about +8% ROA for each 1-point rise on a 7-point deployment scale. The effect was larger where competition was intense and customer preferences changed fast. Deployment depended on five enablers working together: top-management support, an analytics-supportive culture, appropriate data, IT support and analytics skills. A follow-up study of 418 managers (2014) found that firms systematically underestimate and under-invest in analytics. **Grade: B** (large cross-sectional executive survey, self-reported deployment, so causality is not established).

### Cited Findings
- **Citation.** Germann, F., Lilien, G. L., & Rangaswamy, A. (2013). "Performance implications of deploying marketing analytics." *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, 30(2), 114–128. DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2012.10.001. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v30y2013i2p114-128.html); [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/performance-implications-of-deploying-marketing-analytics/)
- **Theory.** The framework draws on upper echelons theory (top managers shape firm practice) and the resource-based view (firm resources and capabilities). — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/performance-implications-of-deploying-marketing-analytics/); [UTS OPUS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/117149)
- **Sample.** 212 senior executives of Fortune 1000 firms. Greater use of marketing analytics was associated with "favorable and apparently sustainable" performance outcomes. — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/performance-implications-of-deploying-marketing-analytics/); [INFORMS press release, Nov 2012](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Marketing-Analytics-Lilien)
- **Effect size.** A 1-unit increase on the 7-point deployment scale went with about an 8% increase in return on assets (ROA), and the results held across performance measures. **Grade: B.** This is a correlational, cross-sectional estimate and should not be read as causal. — [INFORMS press release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Marketing-Analytics-Lilien); [KDnuggets summary, Dec 2012](https://www.kdnuggets.com/2012/12/marketing-analytics-increase-return-by-8-pct.html)
- **Moderators.** The deployment–performance link was stronger under intense industry competition and fast-changing customer preferences. — [INFORMS press release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Marketing-Analytics-Lilien)
- **Antecedents.** Top-management-team support, an analytics-supportive culture, appropriate data, IT support and analytics skills are all described as necessary for effective deployment. — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/performance-implications-of-deploying-marketing-analytics/); [UTS OPUS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/117149)
- **Follow-up study.** Germann, Lilien, Fiedler & Kraus (2014), "Do Retailers Benefit from Deploying Customer Analytics?", *Journal of Retailing*, 90(4), 587–593. Based on 418 top managers in the Americas, EMEA and Asia, retail gained the most from customer analytics of the eight industries studied. Even so, many retailers did not recognise that potential and under-invested relative to what would be economically appropriate, a gap between perception and reality. **Grade: B.** — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/do-retailers-benefit-from-deploying-customer-analytics-3/); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v90y2014i4p587-593.html)
- **Related evidence (UK).** Cao, Duan & El Banna, "A dynamic capability view of marketing analytics", *Industrial Marketing Management*, 76, 72–83 (online 2018, issue 2019). In a survey of 221 UK firm managers:
  - marketing analytics use improved marketing decision-making and product-development management;
  - those two outcomes in turn drove sustained competitive advantage;
  - data availability and managerial support affected outcomes only indirectly, through decision-making (and product development).

  **Grade: B−** (cross-sectional survey). — [REF 2021 record](https://results2021.ref.ac.uk/outputs/5e315b11-5c67-4f6e-9a1b-ba883440fd10?page=1); [Ajman University record](https://research.ajman.ac.ae/en/publications/a-dynamic-capability-view-of-marketing-analytics-evidence-from-uk/)

### Inferences
- **The leader's own behaviour is the first lever.** In upper-echelons terms, the leader is the "top management" for this team. Germann et al. found that leadership support matters, but only together with culture, data, IT and skills. Advocacy alone ("데이터 중요해요") is unlikely to work. The leader has to model the behaviour, for example by visibly basing decisions on data in team meetings and asking "what did the data say?" before approving a plan.
- **"이거까지 해야 해?" signals missing enablers, not lazy staff.** Germann et al. treat all five enablers as necessary together, and Cao et al. find that data availability works only through decision-making. If collection feels like extra work with no visible use in decisions, the "culture" and "decision integration" links are broken. Fix: every data item collected should map to a named decision.
- **The payoff argument suits this team's context.** The effect was strongest where competition is intense and preferences change fast, which describes Korean digital and SNS marketing. The leader can use this to explain why the team should bother.
- **The under-investment gap applies to individuals too.** Germann et al. (2014) found managers systematically underestimate analytics' value. Team members will likely underrate it as well, so short, visible wins help: one campaign where data changed the decision and improved results.
- **Scale caveat.** All of this evidence is firm-level (Fortune 1000 or large firms). Applying it to a 3–8 person team is an inference by analogy.

### Gaps
- **Full-text details not verified.** Only abstracts and press releases were available. Still unchecked:
  - the exact path model (whether top-management advocacy works through analytics culture, as the paper is often summarised, or directly);
  - the coefficient sizes;
  - whether ROA came from objective secondary data or self-report;
  - the paper's stated limitations.
- **No meta-analysis found.** No meta-analysis of marketing-analytics deployment and firm performance turned up, so the evidence base remains single cross-sectional surveys plus one quasi-experiment (Berman & Israeli, under Q2).
- **No team-level or Korean evidence.** No study on marketing analytics adoption in small teams or Korean firms was found in this search.

## Q2. What do Mintz & Currim (2013, JM), Pauwels et al. (2009, marketing dashboards), Wedel & Kannan (2016, JM) and the CMO Survey (Moorman) say about how marketers actually use metrics and why analytics go unused?

### Takeaway
Across two decades of evidence, marketers use available analytics in only about a third to a half of decisions:
- **CMO Survey:** about 30% → 37.5% → 42% → 48.9% at the all-time high.
- **Gartner 2022:** 53% of decisions influenced by marketing analytics.

The main reasons for non-use are organisational, not technical: missing processes and tools to measure analytics' value, a lack of people who can bridge analytics and marketing practice, data-quality problems, and cognitive bias or cherry-picking. Data complexity or timeliness are not the main reasons.

Metric use is explained more by firm strategy, metric orientation and activity type than by individual managers' traits (Mintz & Currim 2013). More metric use goes with better marketing-mix performance, although replications show the relationship is not linear.

The best causal evidence (Berman & Israeli 2022) shows that adopting a descriptive dashboard raises weekly revenue by 4–10%, but only for retailers that actually use it.

### Cited Findings

**Mintz & Currim (2013), JM — Grade B**
- **Citation and design.** "What drives managerial use of marketing and financial metrics and does metric use affect performance of marketing-mix activities?" *Journal of Marketing*, 77(2), 17–40 (DOI 10.1509/jm.11.0463). The model links firm strategy, metric orientation, type of marketing-mix activity, and managerial, firm and environmental characteristics to the use of marketing and financial metrics, and links that use to performance. Data: 1,287 marketing-mix activities reported by 439 U.S. managers. — [OUP Learning Link research insight](https://learninglink.oup.com/access/content/marketing-5e-student-resources/baines5e-chapter-5-4-research-insights)
- **Drivers.** Firm strategy, metric orientation, type of marketing-mix activity, and firm and environmental characteristics explain metric use better than managerial characteristics do. — [OUP Learning Link](https://learninglink.oup.com/access/content/marketing-5e-student-resources/baines5e-chapter-5-4-research-insights)
- **Performance.** More metric use is associated with better marketing-mix performance. — [OUP Learning Link](https://learninglink.oup.com/access/content/marketing-5e-student-resources/baines5e-chapter-5-4-research-insights)
- **Brazil replication (contradicts in part).** 245 activities from 163 managers. Firm strategy, metric orientation and marketing financial accountability had a stronger effect on metric use than firm and environmental characteristics. The authors say this contradicts Mintz & Currim. **Grade: C** (master's thesis, smaller n). — [UFRGS LUME](https://www.lume.ufrgs.br/handle/10183/107259)
- **Japan replication (2021).** 667 managers. Metric use improves performance, but "the relationship is not simple": managers who use few metrics tend to fail, yet using many metrics does not guarantee success. **Grade: B−.** — [J-STAGE, Int'l Journal of Marketing & Distribution](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ijmd/4/1/4_41_01/_article/-char/en)
- **16-country global study (Mintz and colleagues).** Customer satisfaction was used in 53% of the marketing-mix decisions analysed. The lead researcher reports that greater overall use of quantitative information or metrics goes with better decision performance, accuracy and quality. **Grade: B−** (journal venue and year not verified from the snippet). — [UTS news release](https://www.uts.edu.au/node/300321)

**Pauwels et al. (2009), marketing dashboards — Grade C (conceptual paper)**
- **Citation and content.** Pauwels, Ambler, Clark, LaPointe, Reibstein, Skiera, Wierenga & Wiesel, "Dashboards as a Service: Why, What, How, and What Research Is Needed?" *Journal of Service Research*, 12(2), 175–189. The paper explains why firms adopt dashboards (key marketing metrics brought together on one display), sets out five stages of dashboard development, and analyses how demand, supply and implementation interact. Its research agenda lists metric selection, relationships among metrics, and "whether dashboards provide sufficient benefits to justify their adoption." — [Özyeğin University repository](https://eresearch.ozyegin.edu.tr/handle/10679/342); [marketingandmetrics.com](https://marketingandmetrics.com/dashboards-as-a-service-why-what-how-and-what-research-is-needed/)
- **Working-paper version.** "Dashboards and Marketing: Why, What, How, and Which Research is Needed?" (MSI Working Paper 08-203). — [Marketing Center Münster](https://www.marketingcenter.de/en/publication/106507)

**Berman & Israeli (2022), causal evidence on dashboards — Grade B (large quasi-experiment)**
- **Main result.** "The Value of Descriptive Analytics: Evidence from Online Retailers," *Marketing Science*, 41(6), 1074–1096. More than 1,500 e-commerce sites adopted a retail-analytics dashboard at different times. A synthetic difference-in-differences analysis estimates a 4%–10% increase in average weekly revenue after adoption. The gains go only to retailers that adopt and actually use the dashboard. — [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2022/the-value-of-descriptive-analytics-evidence-from-online-retailers/); [INFORMS supplement](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/suppl/10.1287/mksc.2022.1352)
- **Mechanism.** The gain does not come from pricing or ad optimisation. It is consistent with the dashboard working as a monitoring device that helps retailers add and control other martech tools (CRM, personalisation, prospecting) and amplifies their value. Product variety, transactions, visitors and repeat-customer revenue all rose; basket size did not change. — [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2022/the-value-of-descriptive-analytics-evidence-from-online-retailers/)
- **Version caveat.** Earlier working papers reported 13–20%, but the published figure is 4–10%. Cite the published number. — [HBS Working Knowledge (earlier abstract)](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/the-value-of-descriptive-analytics-evidence-from-online-retailers)

**Gartner Marketing Data & Analytics Survey (2022) — Grade C (industry survey, n = 377)**
- **Headline.** Marketing analytics influences only 53% of marketing decisions. Organisations below 50% are more likely to report they cannot prove marketing's value. ### Inferences
- **Treat non-use as a system problem.** Mintz & Currim find that organisational factors (strategy, metric orientation, activity type) explain metric use better than individual traits. The leader should therefore fix the system rather than persuade individuals:
  - a shared "metric orientation" (which metrics matter and why);
  - metric sets tailored to each activity type: performance (CPA, ROAS), content/SNS (reach, engagement, saves), brand/CRM (retention, repurchase, NPS).
- **Fewer, better metrics.** The Japan replication shows that more metrics do not guarantee success. Each sub-team should keep a small, decision-relevant metric set ("collect less, use more") so collection does not feel like busywork.
- **Build a formal review process.** Moorman finds only 35% of firms formally evaluate their analytics, and O'Sullivan et al. find reporting frequency carries the performance effect. A fixed review cadence would help: a weekly 30-minute metric review plus a monthly retrospective with a decision log ("data → interpretation → action → result").
- **Grow "bridge" talent.** Moorman's talent finding suggests developing one member per sub-team who can translate data into marketing implications, or having the leader take that role at first.
- **Guard against cherry-picking.** Gartner's cognitive-bias finding fits the complaint that analyses miss the leader's level and direction. A pre-analysis template helps, written before anyone opens the data:
  - the question;
  - the hypothesis;
  - what result would change the decision.

  This is a standard decision-quality practice; no specific study here tested it.
- **Do not demand analytics for everything.** Gartner's diminishing-returns point beyond 50% of decisions, and the CMO Survey trend toward "focusing analytics on the most important problems", suggest prioritising a few high-stakes decisions.
- **A dashboard pays off only if it is used.** Berman & Israeli: benefits accrue only to users, and the dashboard works as a monitoring device. One shared dashboard is worth building only if it is wired into team rituals and action decisions, not just viewed.
- **Use credibility as the motivator.** O'Sullivan & Abela find measurement ability raises marketing's stature in the firm. "Why bother" can be reframed as: this is how our team proves its value and wins budget and autonomy.

### Gaps
- **Wedel & Kannan's organisational recommendations** (on analytics talent, culture and structure) could not be checked beyond the abstract. Nothing should be cited from it beyond the abstract-level description above.
- **Pauwels et al. (2009)'s five dashboard stages** are not named in the available abstracts, so they are not listed. No empirical effect size for that paper exists; it is conceptual.
- **Mintz & Currim (2013) specifics are unconfirmed:** whether marketing metrics are used more than financial ones, the role of metric orientation, and coefficient sizes.
- **CMO Survey wording and dates.** Exact question wording and dates for the 48.9% "projects" figure and the 2024–2026 "76.3%/78.6%" figures could not be checked; the PDFs were blocked (403/DNS).
- **Search not run.** No searches were run for Pauwels (2014), *It's Not the Size of the Data—It's How You Use It* (book), or for Hanssens & Pauwels (2016), "Demonstrating the Value of Marketing" (JM). Both are likely relevant to dashboard and metric selection.

## Q3. What organisational factors make marketers adopt and use analytics (leadership, skills, incentives, data quality, integration into decisions)?

### Takeaway
The evidence consistently points to six factors:
1. **Leadership or managerial support:** Germann 2013; Cao 2019.
2. **Analytics culture and metric orientation:** Germann 2013; Mintz & Currim 2013.
3. **Skills, especially "bridge" talent that connects analytics to marketing practice:** Germann 2013; CMO Survey.
4. **Data availability and quality:** Germann 2013; Cao 2019; Gartner 2022.
5. **IT and tool support:** Germann 2013; Berman & Israeli 2022.
6. **Formal processes that put analytics into decisions and evaluate its value:** CMO Survey; O'Sullivan et al. 2009.

The evidence for each is mostly B/C (cross-sectional surveys). No direct empirical evidence on incentives was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Leadership, culture, data, IT and skills are jointly necessary.** Top-management support, an analytics-supportive culture, appropriate data, IT support and analytics skills are all required for effective deployment (n = 212, Fortune 1000). **Grade: B.** — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/performance-implications-of-deploying-marketing-analytics/)
- **Data availability and managerial support work through decision-making.** Their effects on outcomes are indirect, running through marketing decision-making (and product development); analytics use drives sustained competitive advantage only through these processes (n = 221 UK firms). **Grade: B−.** — [REF 2021 record](https://results2021.ref.ac.uk/outputs/5e315b11-5c67-4f6e-9a1b-ba883440fd10?page=1)
- **Strategy, metric orientation and accountability drive metric use; individual traits matter less.** Mintz & Currim (2013) find firm strategy, metric orientation and activity type outweigh managerial characteristics. **Grade: B.** — [OUP Learning Link](https://learninglink.oup.com/access/content/marketing-5e-student-resources/baines5e-chapter-5-4-research-insights). The Brazilian replication additionally stresses marketing financial accountability. **Grade: C.** — [UFRGS LUME](https://www.lume.ufrgs.br/handle/10183/107259)
- **Talent and evaluation process are the binding constraints.** Firms lack people who can span analytics and marketing practice; only 3.4% of senior marketers say they have the right talent; only 35% formally evaluate analytics' value. Data timeliness and complexity are not the primary barriers. **Grade: C.** — [CMO Survey blog](https://cmosurvey.org/cmo-survey-marketers-to-spend-on-analytics-use-remains-elusive/); [Duke Fuqua, Aug 2017](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/cmo-survey-aug-2017)
- **Data quality and cognitive bias are the top barriers.** Decision-makers cherry-pick data to support decisions they had already made (n = 377, 2022). **Grade: C.** — [Gartner press release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2022-09-15-gartner-survey-reveals-marketing-analytics-are-only-influencing-53-percent-of-decisions)
- **Reporting frequency carries the performance effect.** It mediates the link between marketing-performance-measurement ability and firm performance (n = 157). **Grade: B−.** — [O'Sullivan, Abela & Hutchinson 2009, EJM](https://www.doi.org/10.1108/03090560910947070)
- **Actual use, not adoption, delivers value.** In a staggered-adoption quasi-experiment with more than 1,500 retailers, only those that adopt and use the dashboard benefit, and the benefit comes partly through complementary martech. **Grade: B.** — [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2022/the-value-of-descriptive-analytics-evidence-from-online-retailers/)
- **Managers underestimate the value of analytics.** This perception gap leads to under-investment (n = 418). **Grade: B.** — [Penn State Pure (Germann et al. 2014)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/do-retailers-benefit-from-deploying-customer-analytics-3/)
- **Maturity models list similar enablers.** Branda (practitioner/academic, venue and year unverified) names professional analytics skills, top-management support, data governance, the credibility of the analytics organisation, and analytics adoption within corporate culture. **Grade: C/D.** — [HSTalks](https://hstalks.com/article/7327/download/)

### Inferences
Each factor below maps to a concrete action for the user's team:
- **Leadership → visible use, not speeches.** The leader explains each decision with data and asks members what data they used. This builds the "analytics culture" that Germann treats as necessary.
- **Metric orientation → a one-page "team metric tree".** It links the team's purpose and goals (the leader's 목적·목표) to sub-team KPIs and to the few metrics each member collects. This tackles the misalignment directly. It is an inference from Mintz & Currim's "metric orientation" construct.
- **Skills → targeted upskilling plus templates.** Train analysis skills and provide a standard analysis-to-action template (observation → insight → action → expected metric change). This closes the gap in level and direction between members' analyses and what the leader expects (bridge talent, per Moorman).
- **Data quality → low-friction collection.** Automate or simplify collection (shared sheet or connected dashboard) so it does not feel like extra work. The Gartner and Germann data enablers support this.
- **Integration into decisions → fixed rituals with a decision log.** A weekly metric review, a monthly retrospective and a decision log give the "formal evaluation" that only about 35% of firms have, and match the reporting-frequency mediator.
- **Incentives → recognise data-driven decisions.** Credit improvements found through data in evaluations and meetings. No direct study was found, so this is expert inference (**Grade D**).
- **Accountability → each person owns a metric and the action it triggers.** The Brazil replication's "marketing financial accountability" factor supports this.

### Gaps
- **Incentives.** No empirical study was found that isolates incentives or performance-evaluation design as a driver of marketers' analytics use.
- **Team level and Korea.** No evidence was found on adoption within small marketing teams (3–8 people) or in Korean firms. All evidence is firm-level, mostly from the U.S., UK or global samples.
- **Recent academic work (2020–2026) is thin.** Searches returned few peer-reviewed studies from this period. Most "recent" evidence here is practitioner surveys (CMO Survey 2024–2026, Gartner 2022) or the 2021 Japan replication. Not searched or verified:
  - Moorman & Day (2016), "Organizing for Marketing Excellence" (JM);
  - the AI-era analytics-adoption literature (2023–2026).
- **Moderators by role.** No evidence was found on whether drivers differ by team function (performance vs content vs CRM); Mintz & Currim's "activity type" factor hints at it, but the details are unverified.
