## Q1. What do meta-analyses of team debriefs and after-action reviews (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013, and later updates) show?

### Takeaway
Structured debriefs and after-action reviews (AARs) are among the best-supported, cheapest learning-loop interventions. Average gains are about 20–25% (d = .67, Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013). A 2021 update covering 61 studies found d = .79 (Keiser & Arthur 2021). The effect depends on design. It is larger when the debrief is **aligned** (same participants, focus and level of measurement as the performance being improved), when it uses **objective review media** (records, data, video) instead of memory, and, for teams, when it is **self-led** by the team instead of run as a lecture by an expert.

### Cited Findings
- **[A] Tannenbaum & Cerasoli (2013), *Human Factors* 55(1), 231–245, "Do team and individual debriefs enhance performance? A meta-analysis."**
  - Scope: 46 samples, N = 2,136.
  - On average, debriefs improved effectiveness over a control group by about **25% (d = .67)**.
  - Effects were similar for teams and individuals, for simulated and real settings, and for medical and nonmedical samples.
  - **Alignment** of participants, focus/intent and level of measurement mattered for getting the largest effects.
  - Authors' conclusion: performance can improve by roughly **20–25%** with properly conducted debriefs, which they describe as a relatively inexpensive, quick intervention.
  - Sources: [PubMed 23516804](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23516804/); [Epistemonikos record](https://www.epistemonikos.org/en/documents/62327b87190b146b78b9cda25182d69d9e3f255d) (consistent across records)
- **[A] Keiser & Arthur (2021), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 106(7), 1007–1032, "A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of the after-action review (or debrief) and factors that influence its effectiveness."**
  - Scope: 61 studies, 107 effect sizes.
  - Overall **d = 0.79**. The authors note this is larger than Tannenbaum & Cerasoli's task-performance estimate of d = .67.
  - The authors define an AAR as a systematic technique that turns a recent event into a learning opportunity by combining task feedback, reflection and discussion.
  - Sources: [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343929916_A_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_the_after-action_review_or_debrief_and_factors_that_influence_its_effectiveness); [SUFE academic newsletter summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/369459)
  - Discrepancy: one secondary review gives "270 studies". The abstract says 61 studies and 107 effect sizes. Use 61.
- **[A, via secondary summaries] Moderators reported for Keiser & Arthur (2021):**
  - **Team AARs were most effective when self-led.** Individual AARs did better when a content expert led them, partly because an expert makes sure all learning points get covered.
  - **Objective review media** (video, documents, records) beat memory-based review. Objective media mattered less for individual, expert-led AARs.
  - The most effective combinations were **self-led + aligned** and **self-led + objective media**.
  - **Structure mattered less** than these factors, and less-structured AARs could still work.
  - Sources: [UNL / QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1029&context=qicwdumbrella); [QIC-WD umbrella summary PDF](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20After-Action%20Reviews%20050221.pdf); [IO at Work practitioner summary](https://www.ioatwork.com/how-to-debrief-using-after-action-reviews/) (practitioner source, grade D on its own; consistent with the QIC-WD summary)
- **[A] Keiser & Arthur (2022), *Journal of Business and Psychology* 37, 953–976.** A second meta-analysis tested six more task and training characteristics that strengthen or weaken AAR effectiveness. I could not retrieve its results. — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-021-09784-x)
- **[A] Keiser and colleagues (2024), *Organizational Psychology Review*, systematic review of technology in AARs.**
  - Scope: 91 empirical studies, 113 AARs.
  - Technology was common, but it was used mostly to support the task itself (92%) or to deliver task feedback (52%), not to structure the reflection.
  - Sources: [CiteDrive record, DOI 10.1177/20413866241245314](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/a-systematic-review-of-technology-in-the-after-action-review-or-debrief/); [NYU IFP listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2024/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/20413866241245314/)
- **[B, small n] Smith-Jentsch, Cannon-Bowers, Tannenbaum & Salas (2008), *Small Group Research* 39(3), 303–327, "Guided team self-correction."**
  - Design: U.S. Navy command-and-control teams (Study 1: 25 teams; Study 2: 13 teams).
  - Debriefs organized around an **expert model of teamwork**, in which members self-diagnose against the model, were compared with a less participative, chronologically organized debrief.
  - The expert-model debriefs produced more accurate team mental models (Study 1) and better teamwork processes and outcomes (Study 2).
  - Sources: [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1046496408317794); [UCSD INSTEP tool page](https://psychiatry.ucsd.edu/research/programs-centers/instep/tools-resource/team-development-interventions/guided-team-self-correction.html)
- **[B/C] Ellis and colleagues (Ellis & Ganzach working paper).** The effect of after-event reviews on performance improvement depended on participants' initial self-efficacy in the personal review but not in the filmed review. This suggests the review format interacts with individual differences. — [Tel Aviv University Coller research paper 151](https://en-coller.tau.ac.il/sites/coller-english.tau.ac.il/files/RP_151_Ellis_Ganzach.pdf)

### Inferences
- The debrief literature is the strongest evidence base in this file. Two independent meta-analyses agree on a medium-to-large effect (d ≈ .67–.79), across settings and for both teams and individuals.
- "Alignment" in this literature is technical: the debrief should involve the people who did the work, focus on the outcome being improved, and match the level at which performance is measured. This fits the user's word "alignment" closely. A debrief on a campaign should be held by the campaign team, about that campaign's KPI.
- The **self-led + objective data** combination is the most relevant moderator. The team itself, not the leader, runs the review, and it reviews recorded data instead of opinions or memory. Guided team self-correction (Smith-Jentsch et al.) shows how a leader with expertise can still contribute: supply the **model or framework** members diagnose against, then step back from running the discussion.
- **Application to the marketing team:** Members probably experience data collection as reporting upward ("for the lead") instead of as the input to their own learning loop. Evidence-based reframing:
  - (1) Run a short, recurring, team-led AAR after each campaign or sprint (What did we intend? What happened, according to the data? Why? What will we do next?). Collected data is then consumed visibly, by the team, within days.
  - (2) Keep the debrief aligned to the role. Performance marketers review performance KPIs, content/SNS members review content metrics, and CRM members review retention metrics, against a shared top-level goal.
  - (3) The lead should contribute the "expert model" as a short checklist or framework (for example hypothesis → metric → result → interpretation → next action) and rotate facilitation among members. Lecturing on the right answer should be avoided.
  - (4) Base every debrief on a dashboard or log, never on recall.
  - This turns "do we really have to do this?" into "this is the input to our own Friday review." That is an inference. No marketing-team trial tested it.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether Tannenbaum & Cerasoli's abstract also reports **facilitation** and **structure** as significant moderators. Practitioner summaries often say so, but I could not verify it against the abstract or full text.
- I could not retrieve the results of Keiser & Arthur (2022) on task and training characteristics, such as task complexity or AAR frequency.
- I found no study of AARs or debriefs in **marketing, sales or office knowledge-work teams**. Nearly all primary studies are military, medical, aviation or lab simulations.
- I found no Korean-sample debrief study, so effects under high power distance, where junior members may defer to the leader during a "self-led" review, are untested.

## Q2. What does Kluger & DeNisi (1996) warn about feedback interventions, and what has updated it?

### Takeaway
Feedback is not reliably positive. Across 607 effect sizes, feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), but **more than one-third of them reduced performance**. This was not explained by sampling error or by whether the feedback was positive or negative. Feedback works worse the more it pulls attention away from the task and toward the self (for example "how do I look / where do I stand vs. others / what does the boss think of me"). A 2020 education meta-analysis confirms an average medium effect (d = .48) but large heterogeneity. Information-rich feedback works best, and feedback moves cognitive outcomes more than motivational or behavioral ones.

### Cited Findings
- **[A] Kluger & DeNisi (1996), *Psychological Bulletin* 119(2), 254–284, "The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory."**
  - Scope: 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations.
  - Feedback interventions improved performance on average (**d = .41**), but **over one-third decreased performance**.
  - This variability could not be explained by sampling error, by feedback sign (positive vs. negative), or by the theories existing at the time.
  - Sources: [Article PDF (mrbartonmaths mirror)](https://www.mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf); [Hebrew University record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232458848_The_Effects_of_Feedback_Interventions_on_Performance_A_Historical_Review_a_Meta-Analysis_and_a_Preliminary_Feedback_Intervention_Theory) (consistent across records)
- **[A] Feedback Intervention Theory (FIT), same paper.**
  - Feedback interventions change where attention goes, among three hierarchically organized levels: **task learning**, **task motivation**, and **meta-task processes, including self-related ones**.
  - **Effectiveness decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy, toward the self and away from the task.**
  - Effects are further moderated by task characteristics that "are still poorly understood."
  - Sources: [Hebrew University record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/); [Article PDF](https://www.mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf)
  - Caution: secondary sources often restate this as "people who received feedback did worse than people who received none." That overstates the abstract. Quote the abstract wording: over one-third of the interventions decreased performance.
- **[A] Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:3087, "The power of feedback revisited."**
  - Scope: 435 studies, 994 effect estimates, more than 61,000 learners.
  - Random-effects pooled effect: **d = 0.48** (medium).
  - **Significant heterogeneity**: "feedback cannot be understood as a single consistent form of treatment."
  - The impact "is substantially influenced by the **information content** conveyed."
  - Feedback has a higher impact on **cognitive and motor-skill outcomes than on motivational and behavioral outcomes**.
  - Sources: [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full); [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/d0708ec1b83041839fa99c22b4403c1d)

### Inferences
- Two meta-analyses converge. Feedback helps on average, but its sign is not guaranteed. The dangerous version is feedback that makes people think about themselves (status, competence, the leader's judgment) instead of the work product.
- Wisniewski et al. show that feedback moves skills more than motivation or behavior. This implies that feedback on analysis quality alone will not fix the motivational part of the problem ("why do we have to do this?"). Purpose and goal alignment (see note 03) and visible use of the data (Q1) must do that work.
- **Application to the marketing team:** The lead is a design-thinking educator and PhD candidate. That creates a large expertise and status gap, which makes self-focused interpretation of feedback likely ("my analysis isn't at the professor's level"). Evidence-based guidance:
  - (1) Aim feedback at the **artifact and the next step**, for example "this segment cut hides the CAC difference, so split by channel." Avoid comments that judge the person or compare members ("this isn't the level I expect").
  - (2) Make feedback **information-rich**: what to change and how, not just a grade or "redo it."
  - (3) Separate feedback for learning from evaluation, so the analysis report does not feel like a performance appraisal.
  - (4) Do not expect feedback to create buy-in. That comes from shared goals and from the team using the data itself.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Wisniewski et al.'s moderator-level effect sizes, for example by feedback type (reinforcement/punishment, corrective, high-information) or by direction. They are in the open-access full text, but my fetch was blocked.
- I could not retrieve Kluger & DeNisi's moderator tables (for example the effects of praise, discouraging feedback, normative comparison and goal-setting cues).
- I found no recent (2020–2026) **workplace** meta-analysis that replicates the "one-third negative" finding specifically in office or marketing settings. The 2020 update is educational.

## Q3. What is the evidence for psychological safety and team learning behavior (Edmondson 1999; Frazier et al. 2017)?

### Takeaway
Team psychological safety, a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, predicts team **learning behavior**: asking questions, seeking feedback, discussing errors and experimenting. In Edmondson's 1999 field study, learning behavior **mediated** the link between psychological safety and team performance. Frazier et al.'s 2017 meta-analysis (136 samples) found psychological safety's strongest relationships were with **information sharing and learning behavior**, but most underlying studies are cross-sectional, so causality is not established. Sanner & Bunderson (2015) add that the safety→learning link is **stronger in knowledge-intensive work**, meaning complex, creative or sensemaking tasks.

### Cited Findings
- **[B] Edmondson (1999), *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(2), 350–383, "Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams."**
  - Design: multimethod field study of **51 work teams** in a manufacturing company.
  - Introduced team psychological safety as "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking."
  - Team psychological safety was associated with learning behavior. **Team efficacy was not, once psychological safety was controlled for.**
  - **Learning behavior mediated** the relationship between team psychological safety and team performance.
  - Sources: [MIT-hosted PDF](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Performance/Edmondson%20Psychological%20safety.pdf); [CiteDrive record, DOI 10.2307/2666999](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/psychological-safety-and-learning-behavior-in-work-teams/); [SUFE newsletter summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/365914) (consistent across records)
  - Antecedents (secondary-source detail): **team leader coaching** and **context support** were identified as antecedents of team psychological safety. This rests on one secondary summary, so verify it before citing. — [SUFE newsletter summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/365914)
- **[A] Frazier, Fainshmidt, Klinger, Pezeshkan & Vracheva (2017), *Personnel Psychology* 70(1), 113–165, "Psychological safety: A meta-analytic review and extension."**
  - Scope: **136 independent samples**, over 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups.
  - The authors compared the effect sizes of antecedents. They tested whether task performance and citizenship behaviors depend on psychological safety beyond leader relations and engagement. They also checked whether study design and **national culture** change the results.
  - Sources: [Wiley record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/peps.12183); [ODU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/management_fac_pubs/13/); [MDSOAR repository](https://mdsoar.org/handle/11603/4275)
  - Secondary summaries say the strongest relationships were with **information sharing and learning behavior**. — [CIPD 2024 psychological safety evidence review (scientific summary)](https://www.cipd.org/globalassets/media/knowledge/knowledge-hub/evidence-reviews/2024-pdfs/8550-psychological-safety-scientific-summary.pdf); [psychsafety.com Field Guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/frazier-et-al-2017/)
  - Approximate values from the CIPD table: learning behavior ρ ≈ .62, information sharing ρ ≈ .51–.52, task performance ρ ≈ .24–.43. The PDF layout is garbled, so the mapping of values to outcomes is **uncertain**. Treat these as indicative only. — [CIPD 2024](https://www.cipd.org/globalassets/media/knowledge/knowledge-hub/evidence-reviews/2024-pdfs/8550-psychological-safety-scientific-summary.pdf)
  - Design caveat: most included studies were **cross-sectional**, so the review does not support causal conclusions. ScienceForWork counts 117 studies, which is probably a different unit (studies vs. samples). — [ScienceForWork](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/psychological-safety/)
- **[A, thin moderator base] Sanner & Bunderson (2015), *Organizational Psychology Review* 5(3), 224–243, "When feeling safe isn't enough."**
  - Meta-analysis finding: the psychological safety → learning/performance link is **stronger in knowledge-intensive settings** (complexity, creativity, sensemaking). Safety alone may not spur learning where the task does not demand it.
  - A third-party summary reports about 51 studies from 48 papers (≈3,700 teams). It notes that the moderation tests rest on only 22–27 studies each, so the mechanism is more tentative than the paper's reputation suggests.
  - Sources: [WashU profile record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/when-feeling-safe-isnt-enough-contextualizing-models-of-safety-an/); [psychsafety.com Field Guide summary](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/sanner-bunderson-2015/)
- **[C, authoritative narrative review] Edmondson & Bransby (2023), *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 10, 55–78, "Psychological safety comes of age."** The review finds four dominant research themes: getting things done (performance), **learning behaviors**, improving the work experience, and **leadership** as the builder of a safe climate. It is a thematic review, not a meta-analysis, and reports no pooled effect sizes. — [psychsafety.com Field Guide record](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/edmondson-bransby-2023.md); [Annual Review PDF (psychsafety.com mirror)](https://psychsafety.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/annurev-orgpsych-120920-055217.pdf)

### Inferences
- The evidence that psychological safety goes with learning behavior is consistent (one large field study plus meta-analyses) but mostly **correlational**. It is fair to say that safety is a reliable correlate and plausible precondition of team learning. It is not established that raising safety causes better analysis.
- Edmondson's finding that **team efficacy did not predict learning once safety was controlled** matters here. Training members to be more capable at analysis may not produce learning behavior if they do not feel safe to show half-formed analyses, question the purpose of the data work, or report that a campaign failed.
- Sanner & Bunderson suggest safety matters **most** for complex, ambiguous, creative work. Marketing analysis and insight generation fit that description, while routine data entry does not.
- **Application to the marketing team:**
  - "Do we really have to do this too?" may be an unspoken objection that members do not feel safe to raise openly. When safety is low, the leader sees only compliance, done half-heartedly, instead of a debate about why the data matters and how to collect it.
  - A lead with a strong expert identity (professor, PhD candidate) may lower safety without intending to, if members fear their analysis will be judged against an academic standard.
  - Evidence-consistent moves:
    - (1) Invite open challenge of the data plan itself ("Which of these metrics is useless for your job?").
    - (2) Use the leader-coaching role that Edmondson identifies as an antecedent: ask questions, model admitting your own wrong hypotheses, and treat failed campaigns as data, not as blame.
    - (3) Combine safety with explicit standards (Q4) and recurring debriefs (Q1). Safety alone is "not enough."
  - Korean teams are often described as high in power distance, so this may be an especially important lever. That is an inference only: I could not retrieve Frazier et al.'s national-culture moderator result.

### Gaps
- I could not verify exact ρ values from Frazier et al. (2017) for learning behavior, information sharing and task performance. The CIPD table is garbled and the full text was not accessible.
- Frazier et al. tested national culture as a moderator, but I could not retrieve the result. This matters for applying the findings in Korea.
- I found no meta-analysis from 2020–2026 that updates Frazier et al. with longitudinal or causal designs. The 2023 Annual Review is narrative.
- I found no study of psychological safety in **marketing teams** specifically, and no Korean-sample study of psychological safety and data or analysis practices.
- Edmondson's widely cited "psychological safety × accountability (high standards)" 2×2 framework, often used to argue that safety must be paired with high standards, appears in her books and talks. I did not retrieve a source for it in this session, so it is not listed as a finding.

## Q4. What does research on rubrics, exemplars and worked examples say about making quality standards explicit?

### Takeaway
Making the quality standard visible works. In a 2023 meta-analysis, rubrics produced a moderate positive effect on performance (g = 0.45, k = 21, still positive after correcting for publication bias). Their effects on self-regulation and self-efficacy were small and uncertain. Rubrics work best when the standard-setter **explains both the criteria and how to use them**, and when people go through a **produce → self-assess or receive feedback → revise** cycle. Exemplars (annotated samples of good work) help people grasp tacit standards, especially when paired with rubrics or self-assessment, and when people draft their own work **before** seeing the exemplar. Worked examples (step-by-step expert solutions) give a medium effect for novices (g = 0.48 in math), but **lose value or backfire as expertise grows** (the expertise-reversal effect). Nearly all of this evidence comes from students.

### Cited Findings
- **[A] Panadero, Jonsson, Pinedo & Fernández-Castilla (2023), *Educational Psychology Review* 35(4), meta-analysis of rubrics.**
  - **Academic performance:** g = 0.45, k = 21, 95% CI [0.312, 0.831]. The pre/post analysis gave SMC = 0.38. A moderate positive effect remained **after correction for publication bias**.
  - **Self-regulated learning:** g = 0.23, k = 5. The CI crosses zero, so the effect is not established.
  - **Self-efficacy:** g = 0.18, k = 3, 95% CI [−0.81, 0.91]. This is too uncertain to support any conclusion.
  - Most moderators were **not significant**, including educational level, number of sessions, and use of self- or peer assessment.
  - Sources: [Deusto repository record](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/59022074-df96-4c6a-8c52-3aa513c0ae3e); [NYU IFP abstract listing](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2024/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-023-09823-4/)
- **[A] Panadero & Jonsson (2020), *Educational Research Review* 30, 100329, "A critical review of the arguments against the use of rubrics."**
  - Scope: 27 publications, 93 excerpts, criticisms grouped into six themes.
  - The empirical evidence behind criticisms of rubrics (for example that they narrow learning or stifle creativity) is, with few exceptions, "neither direct nor strong."
  - Many critics relied on anecdote and assumed rubrics are used only for high-stakes summative grading. The authors call for testing these limitations empirically.
  - Sources: [Deusto research portal record](https://portalinvestigacion.deusto.es/documentos/6326545ad50fae52cd317f56?lang=en); [Deustoteka record](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/94a6df03-3c42-49d0-b6c3-696e85585256/full)
- **[A, small k] Jonsson, Panadero, Pinedo & Fernández-Castilla (2025), *Assessment in Education*, "Using rubrics for formative purposes."**
  - Scope: 15 high-quality rubric interventions.
  - The more successful interventions shared two features:
    - (1) Teachers **explained both the rubric's content and how to apply it formatively**.
    - (2) They used a sequence in which learners **produce work, then receive feedback or self-assess, then revise**.
  - Source: [Deustoteka record](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/e382f19f-3011-49b0-8b71-5d578bb63aa1/full)
- **[A, underlying studies mostly weak designs] To, Panadero & Carless (2022), *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 47(8), 1167–1182, "A systematic review of the educational uses and effects of exemplars."**
  - Scope: 40 empirical studies, mostly post-intervention evaluation designs.
  - Exemplars **paired with rubrics or peer/self-assessment** helped students grasp standards and generate internal feedback for self-monitoring. There was **some** evidence of improved performance and self-regulated learning.
  - Key implication: have learners **draft before seeing exemplars**, so they can compare and improve their own work.
  - The authors call for more controlled quantitative studies.
  - Sources: [Deusto research portal record](https://portalinvestigacion.deusto.es/documentos/6326545ad50fae52cd317f47); [NIE Singapore repository record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/entities/publication/62bbeab5-92a9-48b0-b796-fe0378aa936c)
- **[A] Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023), *Educational Psychology Review* 35, article 11, meta-analysis of the worked-examples effect on mathematics performance.**
  - Scope: 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes. A conference poster reports 185.
  - Average effect (robust variance estimation): **g = 0.48** (medium), described as about +18 percentile points.
  - **Correct examples alone** outperformed incorrect examples or mixed correct + incorrect examples.
  - Adding **self-explanation prompts** moderated the effect negatively.
  - The effect held whether examples were used for initial skill acquisition or for practice.
  - Individual effect sizes ranged from −1.80 to 4.66, so heterogeneity was wide.
  - Sources: [Kent State OAKS record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [UDel meta-analysis poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf); [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1)
- **[A] Atkinson, Derry, Renkl & Wortham (2000), *Review of Educational Research* 70(2), 181–214, "Learning from examples: Instructional principles from the worked examples research."**
  - Worked examples present an expert's solution for a learner to study.
  - Effective examples integrate their components, use multiple modalities, and emphasize **conceptual structure through labeling and segmenting**.
  - **Several varied examples** support abstraction and transfer better than a single example.
  - Sources: [UCR-hosted copy](https://assess.ucr.edu/media/741/download); [Cambridge Handbook chapter on the worked-out examples principle](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/workedout-examples-principle-in-multimedia-learning/5A8A07C14A2911C49982D19950EF5307)
- **[A] Kalyuga (2007), *Educational Psychology Review*, "Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction."**
  - As prior knowledge grows, worked examples become **redundant** and problem solving becomes superior.
  - For learners who can self-explain, the explanations built into worked examples add extraneous load.
  - Sources: [Kalyuga 2007 PDF (UKY course copy)](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf); [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3)
- **[C] Kalyuga (2011), "When exploratory learning may not work: a cognitive load perspective."** For novices, unguided discovery creates heavy working-memory load from intensive unguided search and reduces learning. — [IADIS digital library](https://www.iadisportal.org/digital-library/when-exploratory-learning-may-not-work-a-cognitive-load-perspective)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports a clear principle. If the standard lives only in the expert's head, novices cannot hit it. Making it explicit (a rubric), showing it (exemplars) and demonstrating the process (worked examples) all improve performance on average. The rubric effect (g ≈ .45) and the worked-example effect (g ≈ .48) are medium, but both come from education.
- **How** the standard is introduced matters as much as having one. The rubric must be explained, and people must apply it to their own draft and then revise it. Handing over a rubric or a template alone is the weak version. This lines up with the Q1 debrief evidence and the Q2 feedback evidence: the loop is draft → self-check against the explicit standard → targeted, task-level feedback → revise.
- Expertise reversal warns against one template for everyone. Detailed worked examples help junior members. They may annoy or slow down experienced members (for example a senior performance marketer), which could itself feed the "this is a waste of time" reaction.
- **Application to the marketing team:** The gap between "the level and direction I want" and what the team produces is plausibly a **tacit standard** problem.
  - (1) Co-create a short **analysis rubric** with the team (for example: question tied to the shared goal; data source and validity; appropriate segmentation; insight stated as "so what"; specific next action with owner and metric), with 3–4 performance levels per criterion. Co-creation also builds buy-in.
  - (2) Build an **exemplar bank**: one annotated strong analysis and one mediocre one from past campaigns. Have members **draft first**, then compare against the exemplar.
  - (3) For junior members, provide a **worked example**: the lead's own analysis of one campaign, step by step, labeling each step. Fade it as members gain skill. For senior members, use the rubric only.
  - (4) Use the rubric formatively for self-assessment and in debriefs, not as a grading tool. Grading would trigger the self-focused feedback effects from Q2.

### Gaps
- I found **no workplace or marketing-team study** of rubrics or exemplars for analysis or report quality. All quantitative rubric evidence is from students, and worked-example evidence is mostly mathematics.
- I could not verify the original Sweller & Cooper (1985) worked-example study through search results. The worked-example effect is supported above through Atkinson et al. (2000) and Barbieri et al. (2023) instead.
- A 2024 review by Thorne and colleagues (33 articles), reported in a search summary, found that simply providing an exemplar is not enough and that exemplar selection can shift outcomes either way. I could not link it to a verified record, so it is not listed as a finding.
- I did not retrieve moderator-level numbers for fading schedules (how fast to withdraw worked examples) or for exemplar quantity and quality effects.
